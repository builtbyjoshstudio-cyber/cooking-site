The Dough Hydration Calculator: Baker's Percentages, Flour Absorbency Math, and a Tool I Built Without Ever Baking My Own Bread

A free utility for calculating bread, pizza, pasta, and noodle doughs using baker's percentages — with flour-specific absorbency warnings, whole-egg conversions for fresh pasta, and kansui ratios for ramen. Built because the math problem was interesting, even though I don't bake bread myself.

May 14, 2026·8 min read·Built by Josh Studio LLC
The Short Version

A free baker's-percentage calculator for bread, pizza, pasta, and noodle doughs. Flour is locked at 100% and everything else scales as a percentage of that flour weight, with two calculation modes (target total dough weight or target flour mass), eleven flour types across five flour groups, six traditional style presets (Neapolitan, sourdough, baguette, ciabatta, Tokyo ramen, soba), and built-in checks for flour absorbency limits, unleavened noodle doughs, kansui salts for ramen, and whole-egg conversions for fresh pasta.

I Built This Tool Without Ever Baking My Own Bread

I have never baked my own bread. I have never made my own noodles from scratch. I have never rolled fresh pasta. I cook every night, and I have strong opinions about phở and rice and broth and steak doneness and what to keep in a pantry. Bread and noodles I buy.

So this is the first post in the Projects section where I'm not going to pretend to be the expert. The Dough Hydration Calculator exists because the math problem was interesting, not because I needed it myself. I built it for the home bakers who have asked me, over the years, why their pizza dough never matches the recipe and why their bread loaves come out denser than the ones at the bakery. The answer is almost always the same: they're measuring in cups and the recipe was calibrated in grams, or they're using a flour with different absorbency than the recipe assumed, or they're scaling without understanding how baker's percentages actually work.

The tool fixes all three of those problems. The reason it exists is that home bakers shouldn't have to fight the math on top of fighting the dough. The math should be handled, in the browser, in zero seconds, with the right warnings firing when the inputs would produce a structural failure. That's the entire premise.

Why Baker's Percentages Are the Right Framework

In professional baking — actual bakeries, actual pizzerias, actual ramen shops — recipes don't get written in cups and tablespoons. They get written in baker's percentages: the flour is always 100%, and every other ingredient is calculated as a percentage of that flour mass. Water might be 65%, salt 2%, yeast 1%, and so on. A recipe written this way scales infinitely — you can make a 500-gram batch or a 50-kilogram batch using the same percentages, and the dough behaves the same way.

The reason this matters is that cup measurements are unreliable at scale. A cup of flour from a freshly-opened bag and a cup of flour scooped from the bottom of a stale bag don't weigh the same thing. Humidity changes the density. Compaction during scooping changes the density. Two home bakers following the same recipe with the same flour can end up with substantially different doughs because their cups don't weigh the same. Baker's percentages bypass that entire problem by working from weight.

The calculator handles the math two ways depending on what you actually want to do.

Mode A — Target Total Dough Weight. You input the final dough weight you want (say, 36 ounces because you're making four 9-ounce pizza balls). The calculator solves the equation backwards: it sums all the recipe percentages into a global divisor, isolates the base flour weight, and then scales the other ingredients off that flour weight. The total always comes out exactly to your input. This is the mode most home bakers actually want, because they know how much dough their pan or recipe calls for; they don't necessarily know how much flour that requires.

Mode B — Target Flour Mass. You input a specific flour mass (say, 500 grams of specialty flour you have on hand). The calculator treats that flour weight as the 100% baseline and scales the water, salt, and yeast upward proportionally. This is the mode for "I have this much flour, what do I do with it" situations.

Both modes use the same underlying engine. The choice is just which variable is fixed.

Four Edge Cases Most Hydration Calculators Miss

A basic baker's-percentage calculator isn't hard to build. The harder part — and the more useful one — is handling how differently each flour and dough type behaves. Most online hydration calculators treat every dough the same. This one handles four specific edge cases directly.

Flour-specific absorbency warnings. Different flours absorb water at different rates because of their gluten structure, starch content, and processing. A standard hydration range for Tipo 00 pizza flour is roughly 55% to 62%; push past 62% and the dough gets unworkably sticky. All-Purpose flour handles 60% to 65% well; past that you risk structural collapse during the rise. Gluten-Free baking blends are the opposite — they need higher hydration (above 75%) because there is no gluten network to trap moisture, and the starches take more liquid to hydrate properly. Below that and the dough turns dry, crumbly, and prone to cracking.

Whenever you pick a flour type, the expected hydration range updates with it. If you dial in a hydration percentage outside that flour's safe window, the calculator flags what is likely to go wrong — excessive stickiness, collapse, or dry crumbling — without stopping you from experimenting if you want to.

Dropping yeast for unleavened noodles and pasta. Bread and pizza dough use yeast; noodles and pasta don't. Many online calculators leave a yeast line in everything, which doesn't make sense when you're making soba or egg pasta.

When you pick a noodle or pasta flour — buckwheat for soba, semolina for egg pasta, ramen flour — the calculator drops the Yeast row from the output and adjusts the reverse calculation (in Mode A) to omit yeast from the divisor so the ingredient weights stay right.

Pasta isn't bread, and the math shouldn't pretend it is.

Alkaline salts (kansui) for ramen dough. Authentic ramen uses alkaline flour treated with kansui — a mix of sodium carbonate and potassium carbonate — which gives ramen noodles their yellow tint, chewy bite, and resistance to turning mushy in hot broth. The ratio sits around 1% of the flour weight, and labeling that "yeast" on a ramen sheet is simply wrong.

When Ramen / Alkaline Flour is selected, the calculator keeps that 1% math in place and relabels the line "Alkaline Salts / Kansui (1%)" so the output actually matches what goes into the bowl.

Whole-egg conversion for fresh pasta. Traditional Italian fresh pasta is hydrated with whole eggs rather than plain water. Egg yolks add fat, protein, and color; egg whites provide moisture and structure. Total liquid in fresh pasta typically runs around 35–40% of flour weight, and showing that in whole eggs instead of grams of water is far more practical when you're standing at the counter.

When Semolina / Egg Pasta Flour is selected, the calculator adds a note converting the liquid requirement into whole large eggs at the standard baseline of one large egg ≈ 50 grams (or 1.8 ounces). If a batch needs 200 grams of liquid, the note reads roughly four large eggs — a figure you can use right out of the carton.

Those details are small on paper, but they are the difference between a generic percentage table and a tool that respects how different doughs are actually made.

The Eleven Flour Types and Six Presets

The calculator covers eleven specific flour types, organized into five groups in the dropdown:

  • Standard Wheat: All-Purpose Flour, Bread Flour
  • Artisanal & Whole Grains: Whole Wheat Flour, Dark Rye Flour, Spelt / Einkorn
  • Pizza Specialists: Tipo 00 Flour (Neapolitan), High-Gluten / NY Style Flour
  • Noodle & Pasta Specialists: Buckwheat Flour (Soba), Ramen / Alkaline Flour, Semolina / Egg Pasta Flour
  • Alternative Blends: Gluten-Free Baking Blend

On top of those flour types, the calculator includes six Baking Style Presets that snap the hydration slider to the traditional ratio for that style:

  • Neapolitan Pizza — 60% hydration
  • Sourdough Boule — 72% hydration
  • French Baguette — 65% hydration
  • Italian Ciabatta — 82% hydration
  • Tokyo Ramen — 35% hydration
  • 100% Soba Noodles — 40% hydration

The presets matter because they encode the actual industry-standard hydration ranges for each dough type. A ciabatta is supposed to be a wet dough — that's what gives it the open-crumb structure with the irregular holes. A Tokyo-style ramen dough is supposed to be a very dry dough by bread standards — that's what gives the noodles their firm bite. A home baker working without a preset might pick a hydration percentage that's "in range" generally but wrong for the specific dough they're trying to make. The presets fix that with a single click.

The Honest Disclaimer

Hydration ratios and flour absorption rates vary depending on ambient humidity, brand processing, and milling precision. The calculator gives you accurate baker's-percentage math against industry-standard absorbency ranges. Your specific flour, your specific kitchen, your specific climate may behave slightly differently. Adjust flour or water by small amounts during mixing to achieve target gluten structure — the calculator gets you to the starting point, your hands get you the rest of the way.

This is the same principle the rest of the toolkit operates on. The math gets you close. Attention and adjustment get you the rest of the way.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dough hydration?

Hydration is the weight of the water as a percentage of the flour, not of the whole dough. Flour is always the 100% baseline, and every other ingredient is measured against it. A dough with 1,000 g of flour and 720 g of water is at 72% hydration; higher hydration generally means a more open, airy crumb.

How do I work out the flour from a target dough weight?

Add up the percentages and divide. Flour equals the target dough weight times 100, divided by 100 plus the hydration, salt and yeast percentages. For 1,000 g of sourdough at 72% with 2% salt and 1% yeast, the total is 175, which gives 571 g flour, 411 g water, 11 g salt and 6 g yeast.

How much water can my flour take?

Every flour has a practical ceiling. Tipo 00 pizza flour works at roughly 55% to 62%, and all-purpose handles 60% to 65% well before it turns slack and sticky. Noodle doughs stay deliberately stiff, often under 48%, so they can be rolled and cut. The calculator flags a target outside your flour's workable range.

What hydration is fresh pasta dough?

Much lower than bread. Pick semolina or egg pasta flour and the calculator sets hydration to 45%, leaves out the yeast, and warns once you push past about 48%, where the dough gets too soft to roll thin. For egg pasta it also converts the liquid into whole large eggs at about 50 g (1.8 oz) each.
