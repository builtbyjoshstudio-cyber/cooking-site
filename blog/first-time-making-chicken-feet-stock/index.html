My First Time Making Chicken Feet Stock (And Why I Finally Tried It)

I'd never cooked with chicken feet until a friend who raises pasture-raised birds gave me a batch. Here is how I prepped them, what a 3-hour simmer yielded, and what happened when I reduced it by half.

November 28, 2026·3 min read·Josh Tran
The Short Version

I’ve skipped chicken feet my whole life because I don’t trust big factory farms, but a friend who raises birds on rotating pasture with organic feed and no antibiotics gave me a batch to try. I blanched them for 20 minutes to clean them, then simmered them for 3 hours in a 16-quart pot with just onions, carrots, and garlic—no herbs, no peppercorns. It yielded 5.5 quarts plus two ice cube trays of deeply chickeny, collagen-rich stock, and reducing two quarts down to one turned it noticeably velvety.

Why I Finally Tried Chicken Feet

I’ve heard for years that chicken feet are the secret to rich, gelatinous stock, but I had never actually used them. The reason is simple: I don’t trust big factory farms, and if I’m going to simmer feet for hours, I want to know how those birds were raised.

I’m lucky to have a friend who raises chickens the right way—rotating pasture, organic feed, no antibiotics, with animal welfare first. Once or twice a quarter, when he butchers a batch, he doesn’t do much with the carcasses and gives them to me. I usually end up with about ten of them, and they always make the best broth in my freezer.

This time, I asked him for some of the feet too. Since I’d never worked with them before, I treated it as a straight kitchen experiment and left the cameras off.

How I Prepped and Cooked Them

Because feet do a lot of walking, I didn’t want to skip the cleanup. I washed them thoroughly in cold water first, then put them through a 20-minute simmer to make sure any remaining dirt and impurities came off before the actual stock started.

From there, I built the base the way I normally do—sautéing onions, carrots (no celery this time), and garlic in the bottom of my new 16-quart stock pot—then loaded all the feet in and covered them with water.

I intentionally left out peppercorns and herbs. Since this was my first time tasting a stock made strictly with feet, I wanted to know what they brought to the pot on their own without seasoning masking it. I let it go for 3 hours, skimming the surface every 20 minutes.

The Yield and the Reduction Test

After straining everything out, the batch yielded 5.5 (32 oz) deli containers plus 2 ice cube trays for quick pan sauces and weeknight cooks.

Right out of the pot, the stock was impressive—heavily chickeny and packed with collagen. To see how far it could go, I took two of the 32 oz containers (about 64 oz) and reduced them down to roughly 30 oz. As expected, the chicken flavor concentrated hard, and all that extra collagen from the feet gave the reduced stock a noticeably velvety texture you just don’t get from a quick carcass simmer.

If you’ve been curious about chicken feet stock and you have a local farm or source you actually trust, it’s worth the 20 minutes of extra prep. Next time my friend butchers a batch, I might actually turn the cameras on for it.
