Most of Cooking Happens at the Cutting Board (My Knife & Board Habits)

Three Dalstrong Barbarian knives, a heavy end-grain Lionswood teak board, and the three habits that keep both working before anything ever hits the stove.

November 24, 2026·3 min read·Built by Josh Studio LLC
The Short Version

Most of scratch cooking happens at the cutting board. Three Dalstrong Barbarian Series knives on a heavy end-grain Lionswood teak board — paired with quick honing before prep, using your knuckle as a guide fence, and two minutes of board oil once a month — keep everything working.

Recipe blogs love calling dinner a "20-minute meal," but if you actually cook from scratch, you know the truth: most of the work happens before anything ever touches the stove. When I make a big skillet of spicy sausage hash for dinner and three lunches, forty minutes of that cook is just standing at the cutting board dicing carrots, potatoes, shallots, and peppers.

When you spend that much time on the board, you don’t need a 20-piece knife block. You just need a blade that feels right in your hand, a heavy wood board that won’t slide around, and a couple of small habits that keep both in working shape.

What’s on My Board (And Why)

Long before I ever started a cooking channel, I bought three knives from the Dalstrong Barbarian Series right when they came out: the 8-inch Chef’s Knife, the Obliterator Cleaver, and the 12-inch Slicing & Carving Knife. The second I gripped that chef’s knife for the first time, I just thought, this is so nice. The handle has good weight, the blade is well-balanced, and the price is very fair for the quality you’re getting. That’s honestly what I love about Dalstrong in general—everything I own from them is great value for the dollar.

Underneath them is my Dalstrong Lionswood Teak Cutting Board, which I’ve been cooking on for about two years now. For me, two things matter most in a board: it has to be heavy, and it has to be end-grain. End-grain wood is much easier on your knives so your edges last longer, and the weight just feels good when you’re chopping. It also has rubber pieces in each of the four corners on the bottom so there is zero chance of it moving around on the counter, plus two handles on either side that make it easy to move. I have the 16×12 right now, though in my opinion the bigger the board the better—so I definitely plan on picking up their 25×18 at some point.

3 Habits That Keep Everything Working

1. Hit the Steel Before the First Cut

Before a single vegetable gets cut, my chef’s knife gets a few quick passes on a honing steel. Honing doesn’t grind a new edge onto the blade—it just straightens out the microscopic edge that rolls over during normal prep. A rolled edge slips right off onion and pepper skins, and a slipping knife is how people cut themselves.

2. Use Your Knuckle as a Fence

Shallots and mini sweet peppers end up on my board almost every weeknight. When I’m slicing thin, the habit is simple: one finger in front, two fingers behind, and let the flat of the blade ride against your knuckle. Once the knife has a physical guide wall, you aren't guessing where the edge is going.

3. Two Minutes Once a Month (Before the Wood Goes Chalky)

I learned this one after letting an end-grain teak board sit until it went bone-dry, pale, and thirsty. Bringing it back took five minutes of flooding it with food-safe board oil and letting the wood drink until it stopped. Now I just spend two minutes once a month wiping down our everyday board—oil in an X, work it in, buff it off—so it never gets to the point of warping or cracking. One rule that never changes: never use cooking oil on a wood board, because it goes rancid in the grain.
