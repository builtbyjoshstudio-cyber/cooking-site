Why I Skip Non-Stick (Even Ceramic) — And What’s Actually on My Stove

Sprayed-on coatings wear out under high heat and heavy daily use. Bare clad stainless steel and an enameled cast iron Dutch oven handle everything I cook.

November 24, 2026·3 min read·Built by Josh Studio LLC
The Short Version

I skip Teflon and ceramic non-stick pans because coatings wear down under high heat and heavy daily use. Bare clad stainless steel handles searing, fond, and deglazing, and an enameled cast iron Dutch oven handles soups, braises, and homemade stocks.

I recently talked myself out of buying a new set of ceramic-coated hybrid pans. On paper they sounded convenient—decent brand, heavy bottom, easy cleanup. Then I thought about how I actually cook on a Tuesday night and walked away.

I don’t cook on non-stick skillets. Not traditional Teflon, and not the newer ceramic-coated pans either. Any coating sprayed onto a pan is a wear layer, and under high heat and heavy daily use, it eventually breaks down. I don’t like the idea of that stuff coming up off my pan over time, and I don’t want to baby my burner temperature just to stretch another year out of a skillet.

I cook the way my dad taught me—by feel, with a hot pan and no measuring cups. That usually means browning smoked sausage just to build fond on the bottom of the skillet, pulling browned ground beef out so shallots and peppers can fry in the rendered fat, and scraping everything up with a spatula when the broth hits the pan. Coated pans hate that kind of cooking. Bare metal was built for it.

Here is what actually stays on my stove instead.

Bare Clad Stainless Steel (The Daily Workhorse)

Almost every one-pan dinner on the channel is cooked in my Dalstrong Avalon 5-ply stainless steel skillets. Bare 18/10 stainless has nothing layered on top to chip, flake, or wear out. You can get it ripping hot for a real sear, build a dark fond, deglaze it into a glaze, and scrub it clean without worrying about ruining the surface.

The catch is that Dalstrong discontinued the Avalon cookware line, so you can’t easily buy the exact pans you see in my videos anymore. If you are looking for an in-stock clad stainless skillet that cooks the same way, All-Clad is the one I point people to.

I first discovered All-Clad cooking at a friend’s house. The second I picked up their 12-inch D3 fry pan, I just thought, man, this is nice. The pans feel great in the hand, their heat management is superb, and the small design details actually matter when you’re cooking—like the rolled edge and the handle placement, which sits right in that sweet spot (not too low, not too high) so the pan is a pleasure to cook with and move around. Full transparency: the only All-Clad pieces in my own cabinet right now are a tiny half-quart pot and a 3-to-4-inch mini skillet, so they aren't the pans making dinner—but after cooking on my friend's 12-inch D3 fry pan, it’s obvious why people keep their full-size clad skillets for life.

Enameled Cast Iron (For Soups, Braises & Stocks)

The one finished pot we do keep in regular rotation is our Lodge Enameled Cast Iron Dutch Oven. Porcelain enamel isn't ceramic non-stick—it’s glass fused directly onto cast iron in a kiln. It isn't meant to be slick (you still use oil), and it handles long simmers, homemade stocks, and acidic sauces without reacting with the food. Just use wood or silicone spoons and don’t shock a scorching-hot pot with cold water.

You don't need a 12-piece matching set taking up half your cabinets. One heavy bare stainless skillet and one enameled Dutch oven will handle almost everything you cook—and you won't be throwing them in the trash three years from now.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why skip non-stick pans, even ceramic?

Any coating sprayed onto a pan is a wear layer, and under high heat and heavy daily use it eventually breaks down. I don't like the idea of that coming up off my pan over time, and I don't want to baby my burner temperature to stretch another year out of a skillet. Bare metal handles hot-pan cooking.

Is enameled cast iron the same as ceramic non-stick?

No. Porcelain enamel is glass fused onto cast iron in a kiln, not a sprayed-on non-stick coating. It isn't meant to be slick, so you still use oil. It handles long simmers, stocks and acidic sauces without reacting with the food. Use wood or silicone spoons, and don't shock a scorching-hot pot with cold water.

Can I cook tomato sauce in cast iron?

Not for long in bare cast iron. Acidic foods like tomatoes, wine, lemon juice and vinegar strip the seasoning over a long simmer and can add metallic off-flavors. Stainless steel and enameled cast iron are non-reactive, so they're always safe for acidic cooking. That's why my soups and sauces go in the enameled Dutch oven.

What cookware do I actually need?

Not a 12-piece matching set taking up half your cabinets. One heavy bare stainless skillet and one enameled Dutch oven will handle almost everything you cook, and you won't be throwing them in the trash three years from now. If you want an in-stock clad stainless skillet, All-Clad is the one I point people to.
