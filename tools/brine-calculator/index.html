Why brining works

Brining seasons meat all the way through and helps it hold on to moisture during cooking, so a lean turkey breast or pork chop comes out juicier and more evenly seasoned. A wet brine submerges the meat in saltwater; a dry brine rubs salt directly onto the surface and lets it draw moisture in over time.

Both rely on getting the salt ratio right for the weight of meat — too little does nothing, too much makes it inedibly salty. This calculator works out the salt, water, and optional sugar for your protein and weight, and adjusts for the fact that different salt brands have very different densities by volume.

Dry brine calculator

A dry brine skips the water: you rub salt straight onto the surface, and it pulls moisture out and then back in, seasoned. For a dry brine the calculator sets the salt at about 1% of the meat's weight — adjustable to 0.75% for a lighter cure or 1.25% for a stronger one — and shows that amount by weight and by volume for your salt type, since coarse, table, and sea salts differ a lot by the spoonful. Switch to Dry, enter the protein and weight, and you get the exact salt to scatter on. Best for crisp-skinned poultry and steaks you rest uncovered in the fridge.

Wet brine calculator

A wet brine dissolves salt in water and submerges the meat. The calculator uses about 0.6 liters of water per pound of meat (at least 2 liters), enough to keep it submerged, then salts that water to roughly 5% for a standard brine (4% light, 6% strong) — or, if you pick the equilibrium (EQ) method, targets 1.5% of the combined meat-and-water weight, which is much harder to over-salt on a long soak. It returns the salt (weight and volume), the water amount, and optional sugar, all scaled to your protein and weight. Best for lean cuts like turkey breast and pork chops that dry out easily.

Brine formula and salt by weight

Every number the calculator returns comes from three ratios:

Wet brine: water = 0.6 L per lb of meat (2 L minimum), salt = 5% of the water (4% light, 6% strong)
Equilibrium brine: salt = 1.5% of the meat and water combined
Dry brine: salt = 1% of the meat's weight (0.75% light, 1.25% strong)

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a wet brine and a dry brine?

A wet brine dissolves salt in water and submerges the meat; a dry brine applies salt directly to the surface. Dry brining is less messy and crisps skin better; wet brining adds the most moisture. This tool calculates both.

How much salt do I need to brine a turkey?

For a 10-pound turkey in a standard wet brine, about 300 g of coarse kosher salt in 6 liters (6.3 quarts) of water, brined for about 10 hours. The amount scales with the bird's weight, and salt brands differ a lot by volume, so weigh the salt or pick your salt type in the calculator.

How long should I brine meat?

For a wet brine, about an hour per pound for whole birds, chicken pieces and roasts, never under 30 minutes, with chicken capped at 4 hours and turkey, duck and goose at 24 hours. Steaks, chops and seafood are timed by the piece, not the pack: 30 minutes to 1 hour for steaks and chops, 15 to 30 minutes for fish and seafood. Ground meat isn't wet brined. A dry brine takes 1 to 2 hours per pound (at least 1 hour, with turkey, chicken, duck and goose capped at 24 hours), and an equilibrium brine at least as long as a standard one, up to 2 days.

Can I brine pre-seasoned or "enhanced" meat?

It's not recommended. Many supermarket turkeys and pork are already injected with a salt solution, and brining on top of that makes them too salty. Check the label for "enhanced" or a sodium-containing ingredient list before brining.

What is the formula for a brine?

A standard wet brine is salt at about 5% of the water's weight, which is 50 g of salt per liter of water. A dry brine is salt at about 1% of the meat's weight. This calculator uses about 0.6 liters of water per pound of meat (at least 2 liters) to keep it submerged, so a 12-pound turkey takes about 7.2 liters of water and 360 g of salt.

What is an equilibrium brine?

An equilibrium brine uses exactly the amount of salt you want to end up in the food, so it cannot over-salt however long it soaks. The calculator sets it at 1.5% of the combined weight of the meat and the water. It is slower than a standard brine but far more forgiving, so the calculator gives it at least the standard brine's time and up to 2 days. The limit there is the fridge, not the salt: a 1.5% brine doesn't preserve meat, and raw poultry keeps only 1 to 2 days.

How much salt do I use for a dry brine per pound?

About 1% of the meat's weight, which is roughly 4.5 g of salt per pound. That is about one and a half teaspoons of coarse flaky kosher salt but only three quarters of a teaspoon of table salt, which is why weighing the salt is more reliable than measuring by the spoon.
